# PART 1 — DECK OUTLINE (6 slides)

## Slide 1 — Executive conclusion

**Headline:** *The £550m financing solved a liquidity problem. The model says the problem is structural.*

- **£340m of pro forma liquidity bought c.19 months** — not a solution, a window.
- Aston Martin does **not** become self-sustaining within the forecast horizon. It runs short of
  liquidity first, around **February 2028**.
- **Even at a 0% cost of refinancing, FY2030 free cash flow is £(115)m.** No achievable coupon fixes
  this capital structure.
- The 2029 maturity wall is **£1,357m — c.4x** the liquidity the deal created.
- **Recommendation: the financing delayed a restructuring rather than avoiding one.**

*Design note: this slide must stand alone. An interviewer who reads only this should get the whole thesis.*

## Slide 4 — The structural test *(the analytical core)*

**Headline:** *This is not a refinancing problem. It is a business problem.*

**Test 1 — refinancing the 2029 notes across the rate curve (FY2030):**

| Refi rate | Coverage | FY30 free cash flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0.00% (hypothetical)** | 5.89x | **£(115.1)m** |
| 10.375% (existing coupon) | 1.61x | £(255.9)m |
| 20.16% (market-implied) | 0.96x | £(388.7)m |

**Even at a zero-percent coupon, free cash flow is still £(115)m.** Refinancing terms are not the
binding constraint.

> **Caveat, stated on-slide:** 20.16% is the market-implied **distress yield** on the 2029 notes at
> 30 June 2026 — it embeds default probability and is **not** a forecast new-issue coupon.
> The conclusion does not depend on it: the 0% row makes the same point.

**Test 2 — can the business fund its own capex, before any interest?**

| £m | FY26 | FY27 | FY28 | FY29 | FY30 | FY31 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EBITDA less capex | (35) | **(62)** | (51) | (44) | (37) | **20** |

**EBITDA does not cover capex alone until FY2031** — and then by £20m, before a penny of interest.

**Test 3 — the market's own verdict.** The 2029 notes were marked at **79.6% of par** at 30 June 2026,

## Slide 5 — Scenario output: no margin for error

**Headline:** *The base case is already cash-negative. The downside only changes the date.*

| | Base | Downside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covenant breach | FY2027 | FY2027 |
| Liquidity exhausted | **c. Feb-2028** | **c. Nov-2027** |
| Runway from 22-Jul-26 | c.19 months | c.16 months |
| Peak funding gap | £1,319m | £2,163m |
| FY2031 net debt / EBITDA | 10.2x | 29.9x |
| FY2029 coverage | 1.0x | 0.4x |

**The reframing that matters:** the base case is not a comfortable case. It assumes recovery to
**Aston Martin's own best-ever margin (17.1%, FY2024)** and still burns cash every single year. On a
volume-only stress, the model tips into liquidity exhaustion at just **−6.6%** volumes — the base case
sits on the cliff edge, so the story is *no margin for error*, not *the downside is severe*.

Covenant breach occurs in FY2027 at **every** tested threshold (£75m / £100m / £125m), so the date does
not depend on the undisclosed covenant level.

> **Caveat, stated on-slide:** the downside is a **no-response case** — it deliberately excludes the
> mitigating actions management states it would take (capex deferral first). Real mitigation would
> soften it. This matches management's own reverse stress test construction.

## Slide 6 — Alternatives and recommendation

**Headline:** *The right deal available. The wrong problem solved.*

| Option | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Rights issue | Only option that fixes solvency — but c.£1.3bn needed, ≈ FY2025 revenue. Yew Tree already stretched |
| Convertible | Saves cash interest; does not touch the £62m FY2027 EBITDA-less-capex deficit |
| Public secured bond | Effectively unplaceable — existing notes at 79.6% of par |
| **Private credit (actual)** | Fast, certain, flexible. +£31m interest, more secured claims, bought c.19 months |
| Mixed debt / equity | Only structure addressing both liquidity and the structural deficit |

**Recommendation — outcome 3 of 3: the financing delayed a restructuring rather than avoiding one.**

Two separable judgements, and the distinction matters:

1. **As an execution decision, the deal is defensible.** With the public market shut, a stretched
   shareholder base and an urgent need, private credit was plausibly the only executable option.
2. **As a solution, it does not work.** It addressed liquidity; the deficit is structural.

**What would have to be true for the softer "bought sufficient time" verdict:** EBITDA of **£651.7m**
by FY2031 — more than double the modelled £320m, implying c.£2,823m of revenue and c.9,870 units
against a **FY2024 peak of £271m on 6,030 units**. That is roughly 1.6x the best year in the company's
recent history. Possible, but it is a bet on operational transformation, not on the financing.

**Expect a further capital event — equity, asset sales or a liability-management exercise — well before
the March 2029 maturity.**

# PART 2 — MEMO DRAFT (one page)

**TO:** Investment Committee
**FROM:** [Analyst]
**DATE:** August 2026
**RE:** Aston Martin Lagonda — £550m private credit financing: solved, or postponed?

### Recommendation

The July 2026 financing **delayed a restructuring rather than avoiding one**. It solved a liquidity
problem; the analysis indicates the problem is structural. We expect a further capital event — equity,
asset sales or a liability-management exercise — **before the March 2029 maturity**.

### Situation

At 30 June 2026 Aston Martin carried **£1,544.7m of net debt** against **£145.2m of total liquidity**,
adjusted net leverage of **8.9x**, an H1 adjusted EBIT loss of **£108.9m** and an H1 free cash outflow
of **£197.6m**. The £170m RCF was fully utilised. On 22 July the Group closed a **£450m senior secured
term loan plus a £100m delayed-draw facility** at **SONIA + 6.75%**, maturing July 2031, led by HPS
Investment Partners, repaying and cancelling both the RCF and the £20m drawn Yew Tree facility. Pro
forma liquidity rose to **c.£340m**.

### Analysis

**The financing does not change the trajectory.** On a base case capped at Aston Martin's own best-ever
EBITDA margin (17.1%, FY2024) and carrying its own guided £1.7bn capex programme, free cash flow is
**negative in every forecast year**. Liquidity is exhausted around **February 2028** — a runway of
roughly **19 months** — with a covenant breach in **FY2027** at every plausible threshold.

**Refinancing terms are not the binding constraint.** Testing the 2029 maturity across the rate curve,
**even a hypothetical 0% coupon leaves FY2030 free cash flow at £(115)m**. The company does not cover
**capex alone** until FY2031, and then by only £20m — before any interest. The 2029 wall of **£1,357m**
is c.4x the liquidity the deal created.

**The market has already priced this.** The 2029 notes were marked at **79.6% of par** at 30 June 2026,
down from 92.6% in December and *before* this financing was announced — an implied yield of c.20%,
which prices a restructuring rather than a refinancing. *(This is a distress yield reflecting default
risk, not a forecast new-issue coupon.)*

**There is no margin for error.** On a volume-only stress the base case tips into liquidity exhaustion
at a **−6.6%** volume decline. A combined downside — anchored on management's own disclosed reverse
stress test — brings exhaustion forward to **c.November 2027** and lifts the peak funding requirement
from £1.3bn to £2.2bn. *(The downside excludes the mitigating actions management states it would take;
real mitigation would soften it.)*

> **Market context (per Bloomberg reporting; not confirmed by Aston Martin):** the financing was
> reported as a drop-down structure that subordinates existing bondholders, with a creditor group led
> by Arini, BlackRock and Sculptor opposing it and retaining Jefferies. Not relied upon in any figure
> or conclusion above.

### Assessment of the decision

Two judgements should be separated. **As execution**, the transaction is defensible: with the public
market effectively shut, a shareholder base already stretched and an urgent need, private credit was
plausibly the only option available in the window. **As a solution**, it does not work — it bought
c.19 months at a cost of **+£31m of annual interest**, additional secured claims ranking ahead of
existing bondholders, and a materially more encumbered balance sheet.

### Key risks to this view

The forecast beyond FY2026 rests on drivers **Aston Martin has not guided**; our base case is
deliberately more conservative than management's undisclosed plan, and the conclusion is sensitive to
it. For the softer "bought sufficient time" verdict to hold, FY2031 EBITDA would need to reach
**£651.7m** — more than double our modelled £320m, and c.2.4x the company's FY2024 peak of £271m.
